Bitwise & I/O Operators

Working directly on the 0s and 1s inside a number — and a final look at the two arrows << and >> that you have been using since Lesson 1.

By the end of this lesson you can
  • write a number in binary and apply &, |, ^, ~ bit by bit
  • use << and >> as shifts: multiply and divide by powers of 2
  • explain why << and >> also work as insertion and extraction operators
First, the picture

Inside the computer, a number is a row of switches

Picture it

Eight light switches in a row. Each is ON (1) or OFF (0). Each switch is worth a different amount, doubling from right to left. The number is the sum of the switches that are ON.

1286432168421Number
000011008 + 4 = 12
000010108 + 2 = 10
Trick

Place values: 128 64 32 16 8 4 2 1. To write a number, go left to right: if it fits, put 1 and subtract; otherwise put 0.

Bitwise operators work on each bit separately

&   |   ^   ~

& AND

1 only if both bits are 1.

Both switches must be ON for the light to shine.

| OR

1 if at least one bit is 1.

Any switch ON lights it.

^ XOR

1 if the bits are different.

Like a staircase light: flipping either switch changes it.

~ NOT

Flips every bit (0→1, 1→0). Unary.

Also called the one’s complement.

aba & ba | ba ^ b~a

AND: both 1  ·  OR: any 1  ·  XOR: different  ·  NOT: flip

How to read it

AND: only the 8-column has 1 in both rows → 1000 = 8.

OR: columns 8, 4 and 2 have at least one 1 → 1110 = 14.

XOR: columns 4 and 2 are different (the 8-column matches) → 0110 = 6.

  1. a & b = 8, exactly what our column-by-column table gave.
  2. a | b = 14.
  3. a ^ b = 6. The brackets are needed because << has higher precedence than &, | and ^ (Lesson 7 has the full table).
The NOT operator

~ flips every bit — and gives −(n + 1)

5 in 8 bits → flip everything

0000 0101   (5)
1111 1010   (~5)

In a signed number the leftmost bit means “negative”. The pattern 1111 1010 is the number −6.

~n = −(n + 1)   i.e.  ~5 = −6,  ~0 = −1,  ~(−1) = 0
Exam shortcut

No binary needed: flip the sign and subtract 1.
~9 → −9 − 1 = −10

Sliding the bits

Shifts slide all the bits left or right

Left shift x << n

Slide bits left by n, filling with 0s on the right.

Multiplies by 2n.

3 << 1 = 6   3 << 2 = 12

Right shift x >> n

Slide bits right by n; the bits that fall off are lost.

Divides by 2n (drops the remainder).

40 >> 1 = 20   13 >> 2 = 3

Seeing the slide: 3 << 2

0000 0011   (3)
0000 1100   (3 << 2 = 12)

The two 1s moved two places left. Each place left doubles the value.

<< multiplies by 2 each step  ·  >> divides by 2 each step
Turbo C++ note

Turbo’s int has only 16 bits, so a left shift can push a 1 off the end sooner (e.g. 1 << 15 becomes negative). Modern int has 32 bits.

See it work

Left shift multiplies, right shift divides

  1. x = 3, y = 40.
  2. Left shifts: 3 × 2 = 6, 3 × 4 = 12, 3 × 8 = 24. Each extra shift doubles again.
  3. Right shifts: 40 ÷ 2 = 20, 40 ÷ 4 = 10, 40 ÷ 8 = 5. Each extra shift halves again.
Why programmers love bits

Three classic tricks

Odd or even

n & 1 looks at the last bit: 1 = odd, 0 = even.

Swap without a spare

a = a ^ b;
b = a ^ b;
a = a ^ b;

Quick × and ÷

n << 1 doubles, n >> 1 halves. Faster than * and / on some machines.

  1. 13 = 1101. The last bit is 1, so 13 & 1 = 1: the number is odd.
  2. XOR swap. a = 5 ^ 9 = 12. Then b = 12 ^ 9 = 5 (the old a). Then a = 12 ^ 5 = 9 (the old b). The numbers swapped with no third variable!
  3. Result: a = 9, b = 5.

Remember

Single symbol = bits. Double symbol = logic.

The two arrows you already know

<< and >> have two jobs each

Picture it

The English word “bat” can mean a cricket bat or a flying animal. You decide the meaning from the context. C++ does the same with << and >>: the left-hand side decides what the symbol means.

SymbolLeft side is a numberLeft side is cout / cin
<<Left shift
x << 2		Insertion operator
cout << x — inserts data into the output stream
>>Right shift
x >> 2		Extraction operator
cin >> x — extracts data from the input stream

Why “insertion”?

Output is a stream flowing to the screen. << pushes (inserts) values into it, and it chains: cout << "a" << 5 << endl;

Why “extraction”?

Input is a stream coming from the keyboard. >> pulls (extracts) values out of it into variables, and also chains: cin >> a >> b;

Remember

The arrows point the way the data flows: into cout, out of cin into the variable.

See it work

The same << in two roles

  1. Insertion: cout << "..." — cout is on the left, so << inserts text into the output.
  2. Extraction: cin >> a — pulls the typed number out of the input into a. (Sample run: the user types 5.)
  3. Both roles in one line. The first and third << are insertion (after cout). But (a << 2) has a number on the left, so it is a left shift: 5 × 4 = 20. The brackets stop the compiler getting confused.

Lesson 6 recap

What we learned

✅ Bitwise

  • AND: both 1  ·  OR: any 1  ·  XOR: different  ·  NOT: flip.
  • ~n = −(n + 1).
  • << multiplies by 2 per step, >> divides by 2 per step.
  • Single symbol = bits, double = logic.

✅ Insertion & extraction

  • cout << x inserts into the output stream.
  • cin >> x extracts from the input stream.
  • Both arrows can be chained.
  • With a number on the left they are shifts; put shifts in brackets inside cout.
